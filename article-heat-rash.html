Beat the Hapdi: Preventing and Soothing Heat Rash

CL Dr. Camille Lim, MD Sep 28, 2026 5 min read Heat & Climate

🔑 The short version

  • Heat rash (prickly heat, butil) happens when sweat ducts get blocked and sweat traps under the skin.
  • Fast relief: cool shower, pat dry, loose cotton clothing, and keep the area exposed to air.
  • Most cases settle in 3 to 7 days on their own.
  • See a doctor if there is fever, pus, spreading pain, or if it does not improve within a week.

That prickly, stinging itch that shows up on the neck, chest, back or skin folds during the hottest months has a medical name — miliaria — and a very ordinary cause: sweat that cannot get out.

In tropical heat, sweat glands work overtime. When sweat sits on the skin — blocked by tight clothing, heavy creams, constant humidity or simply not drying off well — it irritates the duct and leaks into nearby skin layers. The immune system reacts, and you get the familiar crop of tiny itchy bumps.

The four types you'll meet

TypeWhat it looks likeWhat to do
CrystallicTiny clear blisters, no itch; common in infantsUsually clears alone within days
ClearSmall clear bumps, strong itch, comes and goes with heatCool down, keep dry, use calamine
Red (rubra)Red itchy bumps, worse with friction — the classic "prickly heat"Reduce friction, loose clothes, anti-itch care
Deep (profunda)Firm flesh-colored bumps deeper in the skin, on the trunkNeeds medical assessment — can persist

Fast relief when it flares

  1. Cool the area. Take a cool (not ice-cold) shower and let the water run over the rash for a minute or two.
  2. Pat dry — don't rub. Rubbing spreads irritation and breaks the surface of the bumps.
  3. Expose it to air. Skip the jacket, choose a loose cotton shirt, and if you're at home, let the area breathe for a while.
  4. Soothe the itch. Calamine lotion or an over-the-counter anti-itch gel helps most people. For stubborn itch, a short course of hydrocortisone 1% can be used for a few days — but not on the face, broken skin or for children without advice.
  5. Leave the bumps alone. Scratching ruptures the ducts further and can introduce bacteria.

⚠ Avoid these "home remedies"

Calamansi, vinegar, baking soda and alcohol all sting on irritated skin and can worsen the rash or cause a chemical burn. Baby powder in the folds looks tidy but can be inhaled by infants and does not treat the cause — dryness and airflow do.

Prevention that actually works

  • Shower soon after sweating. Sitting in a damp uniform for hours is the fastest route to a flare.
  • Wear loose, breathable fabric. Light cotton or moisture-wicking material beats tight polyester in the heat.
  • Dry every fold. Neck, armpits, under the breasts, groin and between toes — moisture in folds is the usual starting point.
  • Skip heavy creams and oils in hot weather. Rich occlusives trap heat and block ducts. Use a light, non-comedogenic lotion where you do need moisture.
  • Cool your sleeping space. Night sweats are a common trigger; a fan, lighter sleepwear and thinner sheets help more than any powder.
  • Give skin a break. If a mask, helmet or backpack strap sits on the same area daily, pad it or alternate routes and gear.
"In my clinics the pattern is always the same: April to May, plus any week with a brownout. The skin isn't weak — it's blocked sweat plus friction plus heat."
— Dr. Camille Lim, MD, dermatologist

When to see a doctor

See a healthcare professional if any of these appear:

  • Fever, chills or a feeling of being generally unwell along with the rash.
  • Pus, spreading redness, warmth or increasing pain — signs of a bacterial infection on top of the rash.
  • No improvement after a week of consistent care, or repeated flares every few weeks.
  • In babies and older adults, or anyone with diabetes — heat rash is easier to complicate and worth checking early.
This article is for general education and is not a substitute for personal medical advice. Medications and lotions mentioned should be used as directed on the label, and any treatment for infants or during pregnancy should be cleared with a clinician first.
