Mosquito Bites and Dengue Season: A Practical Protection Guide

RS Rafael Santos, RN Aug 22, 2026 5 min read First Aid

🔑 The short version

  • Aedes mosquitoes — the dengue vector — bite during the day and breed in small containers of clean water.
  • Use repellents with DEET (20–30%), picaridin or IR3535; apply over sunscreen, reapply as labeled.
  • Empty, scrub and cover water-holding containers once a week — that breaks the breeding cycle.
  • Don't scratch bites; watch for dengue warning signs during fever and seek care immediately.

Mosquito bites are the everyday nuisance of tropical life. But in a country where dengue, chikungunya and Zika circulate, protection is also a health measure — and it works best when it covers three fronts: repel, remove breeding sites, and know when to seek care.

Choosing and using repellent

Repellents do not kill mosquitoes; they make you harder to find. The ingredients with the strongest evidence for daytime outdoor protection are:

  • DEET (20–30%) — long track record, effective for hours. Works under sunscreen.
  • Picaridin (20%) — comparable to DEET, lighter feel, does not damage plastics.
  • IR3535 — a reliable alternative, common in family products.
  • Oil of lemon eucalyptus — plant-derived option for adults; generally not recommended for children under three.

🧴 How to apply it properly

Apply sunscreen first, then repellent over it. Use on exposed skin only — avoid eyes, mouth, hands of small children, and any cut or irritated skin. Outdoors, reapply according to the label rather than on a timer. When you're back indoors, wash it off with soap and water.

Protect the house: remove the nursery

Aedes mosquitoes rarely fly far from where they hatched. A single bottle cap of standing water can hold dozens of larvae, which is why weekly source reduction beats fogging for household protection.

  • Turn over or store old tires, pails, bottles and wheelbarrows.
  • Empty and scrub plant saucers, vases and pet water bowls at least weekly — scrubbing removes eggs that stick to the surface.
  • Cover water storage drums and tanks tightly; keep roof gutters clear.
  • Screen windows and use bed nets if screens are missing, even during daytime naps.
  • Manage waste: bagged trash and covered bins, checked regularly.

Wear light-colored, loose long sleeves at dawn and late afternoon for outings, since dark colors and exposed skin are more attractive to many mosquito species.

Caring for a bite that's already itchy

  1. Wash the area with mild soap and cool water.
  2. Apply a cold compress for 10 minutes — it reduces swelling and the itch signal.
  3. Use an anti-itch product such as calamine lotion or a 1% hydrocortisone cream for a short period.
  4. Keep nails short and resist scratching: broken skin is how secondary bacterial infection starts, which is a far bigger problem than the bite itself.
  5. Watch the bite for increasing redness, warmth, pus, swelling or red streaks — signs to see a clinician.

⚠ Dengue warning signs — seek care the same day

During or after a fever, get emergency help for: severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from the gums or nose, blood in vomit or stool, extreme fatigue and restlessness, or a rapid drop in temperature as the fever breaks. Avoid aspirin and ibuprofen until dengue has been ruled out by a professional — acetaminophen/paracetamol is the usual pain/fever option when advised.

"Mosquito control is not one loud operation — it's a boring weekly habit of tipping over water. The households that never skip it are the ones that stop having cases."
— Rafael Santos, RN, community health nurse

A small outdoor kit worth carrying

  • A bottle of repellent — small enough to live in your bag.
  • Light long sleeves for evening errands.
  • Calamine or anti-itch cream, plus a few adhesive bandages.
  • Oral rehydration salts — useful in heat and during fevers.
This article is for general education and is not a substitute for personal medical advice. Fever with any of the warning signs above requires prompt medical assessment at the nearest clinic or hospital.
